# GAME APPLICATION - DEPARTMENT OF SOFTWARE ENGINEERING

A playable single-player and 2–4 player cooperative graphic-novel third-person stealth prototype by Mirhazar Khan, using the original MIT-licensed Spirit particle simulation by Edan Kwan. The industrial references guide the atmosphere and mechanics. This is a complete three-hub prototype loop, not yet a finished 1–2 hour game or a match for the reference renders' production assets.

## Multiplayer: no one left behind

Open `START_HERE.md` for the ready-to-play launcher and `MULTIPLAYER.md` for the complete crew loop. One player creates a room, friends open the same game URL and join with its six-character code, everyone selects Ready, and the host launches. The title's living GPU field transitions directly into each player's spirit. Crimson, cyan, violet and amber distinguish the crew; a requested color that is already taken is assigned another available hue.

The mission requires different players to hold both lobby relays, then both raised security relays. Download one shared data key and reach rooftop extraction together. Hold F beside a captured teammate to restore their field. Nearby spirits form a cover-aware resonance link that improves cohesion. G casts a colored echo lure; V marks a route. X shares sentinel marks, while two simultaneous scans slow the shared world. Individual dash aiming stays live so one player cannot freeze everyone else's game.

Rooms and recovery snapshots live in the hosted D1 database or the downloadable LAN server's SQLite file. Direct WebRTC channels carry compact emitter/stance snapshots and reliable actions; addressed signaling and a same-origin HTTP relay recover when a direct link cannot open. The elected player runs patrols and heist rules; host changes preserve the last shared world. Individual particle positions are never transmitted. Local spirits retain WebGPU compute with the original WebGL path as fallback; teammates use independent copies of that same curl/facet GPU shader with distance-based particle counts. Teammates' mimicry, wall contact, trails, phase transit and consume shells are visible to the crew.

This is a cooperative prototype for trusted players. It uses an elected peer for world simulation, rather than a dedicated competitive anti-cheat server. The hosted page retains its existing private access settings; a room code does not grant website access. The included LAN launcher lets classmates play from the same Wi-Fi without changing website sharing.

## Play

The minimal title screen credits **MIRHAZAR KHAN** and runs the same original Spirit GPU field as the game. Mouse movement projects an emitter into its 3D plane; the existing curl, attraction, lifetime renewal and smoky trails remain active. **Start** becomes available once the renderer is ready, then carries that living field into the player position while the map fades in and the camera moves into third-person view. The 1.35-second handoff retains particle buffers, curl origin and simulation time; patrols and movement stay frozen until it finishes. Reduced-motion preference shortens the camera/reveal transition. Gameplay controls appear on arrival.

Start requests pointer-lock mouse look immediately; if the browser declines, drag the scene to look, or click it to try again. WASD/arrows follow the camera's rendered horizontal direction, including detours around cover. Acceleration, braking and reversal are smoothed with a frame-rate-independent response. Mouse orbit is damped, and the camera tracks the actual particle emitter and trailing plume. Wheel changes camera distance. Shift produces a faster, brighter, audible burst with smooth speed blur. A subtle measured shutter follows ordinary movement and camera rotation. Shift blur strengthens with actual travel speed and fades on release; pressing Shift while stationary or blocked does not create a speed burst. Low vapor and wall traversal retain only the subtle travel shutter. Hold C to flow low through the shutter gap and beneath laser bars. On touch devices, use the directional pad, drag the scene, and tap the ability controls; Low Vapor toggles.

Face a solid wall, pillar, partition, server rack or rooftop building and press Q to attach. W/S ascend and descend; A/D slide sideways along the face. Clear ledges automatically transition onto the top, which supports ordinary walking. Q releases the wall, and descending to ground releases it naturally. Ceilings, adjacent obstacles and map edges constrain traversal. Wall attachment flattens the same GPU particle field against the surface; it is a movement state, so guards can still see you. Low vapor, phase aiming and consumption resume after releasing the wall.

E mimics the nearby shimmering node you face, or a nearby flat wall; E releases the disguise. F operates nearby terminals. Hold Space to slow time to 12%, aim the camera at a clear destination and release for a 0.30-second dash up to 36 metres along a single straight direction line, with stronger speed blur and flickering crimson lightning trailing the transit. The existing particle form travels swiftly to the endpoint with its ordinary curl animation; there is no range circle or dash deformation. X slows world time to 65% and fades the opaque environment into a charcoal scan, with visible and occluded enemies highlighted red. R faces and consumes a nearby sentinel across a clear corridor: tendrils reach out, the vapor envelops its body, and its core collapses. The 1.35-second sequence restores 24 cohesion, has an 8-second cooldown, and makes enough noise to draw other guards. P/Escape pauses. The top-right particle menu includes Auto, 4K, 8K, 16K, 32K, 65K, 131K and 262K. Auto and settings up to the allocated capacity change immediately without restarting; increasing beyond that capacity reloads the mission. Choices are saved on the device.

## Infiltration loop

The lobby offers patrol observation, wall shadows, vase/crate/drone/extinguisher nodes, a security shutter and an override terminal. Low vapor can pass under the closed shutter. Entering the data core establishes a checkpoint.

The data core contains server aisles, actual stairs, an elevated gallery and crosswalk, camera sweeps, a drone and a laser grid. Use low vapor, phase across clear space, or reach the laser-control terminal on the western gallery. Extract the key from the vault terminal, then pass around it to the rooftop stair shaft. The key establishes another checkpoint.

The rooftop opens to the city skyline with equipment cover, patrols, a drone and more mimic nodes. Reach the extraction pad with the data key and low suspicion. Capture retries the most recent checkpoint while preserving terminal and key progress. Winning or starting anew resets the mission.

## Mimicry and detection

Six solid objects—crate, vase, terminal, extinguisher, stationary cleaning drone and light fixture—have opaque, textured geometry. GPU particles rush onto their targets from the outer shell inward before the solid model resolves. A faint smoky pulse remains. Wall mimicry becomes an animated shadow texture on the actual surface.

Solid forms are immobile. Complex forms drain a full cohesion meter in 18 seconds; the simpler wall shadow lasts 22 seconds. Harsh security beams add rapid drain and brighten vapor. Ambient room light is safe. At zero cohesion, mimicry collapses. Vapor recovers cohesion in shadow.

Hovering robotic alien sentinels—with metal gimbals and emissive slit-pupil eyes—detect moving vapor faster than stationary vapor. Distance, ambient exposure, low stance and the visible fraction of the silhouette affect recognition speed. Low vapor stays quiet even when Shift is held. Burst and dash sounds create local investigation events; solid cover muffles their range. A witnessed transformation remains recognized until the observer loses sight. A guard bumping into a solid disguise raises an alert. Cameras and drones use 3D beams and remember a field they observe. Walls, floors, ceilings and props occlude sight and beams. Sentinels patrol, investigate a sound or observed signature, search around the last known position, and return to their route after losing the target. They do not track an unseen player through walls. Capture requires actual visibility. A shared navigation grid caches routes around machinery, columns and buildings; it respects floor elevations and the shutter.

## GPU implementation

**WebGPU compute:** WGSL compute dispatch updates ping-pong storage buffers for position/lifetime/velocity. Keyboard input moves the emitter, using the original Spirit mouse-follow model: living particles keep their world-space positions, follow a common attraction point with the original life-dependent `0.005 + life * 0.01` force and distance falloff, and receive the original three-octave analytic 4D simplex curl. Moving vapor retains the original lifetime decay of 0.015 per 60 Hz step at normal walking speed; decay follows the smoothed movement envelope and continues at 0.006 per step when idle, renewing the cloud. Vapor births fill an amorphous ellipsoid rather than a humanoid atlas. Living vapor follows a common emitter and keeps its individual world-space history at every movement speed. Birth orientation follows the latest movement direction. A directional emission offset and movement-gated breathing feed the plume. Vertical climbing drives the same world-space history and curl, leaving a trail below the moving field. Older particles linger on the route, bend through turns and fade; fresh particles form around the moving emitter. Stopping smoothly reduces turbulence to a gentle continuous flow. Curl, lifetime fading and particle renewal stay active while idle; the cloud remains amorphous and cohesive without returning to a body silhouette. The force, emission radius and curl magnitude are scaled to the game's scene units. Low stance compresses the field beneath the shutter, and local box contact avoids cover.

A phase dash transports the mass and its curl-field origin together, retaining the ordinary animation. Normal movement keeps the field anchored in world space. CPU code uploads control uniforms and collision geometry; it never reads or animates particle positions. Storage-buffer vertices render the original alternating triangular billboard facets, with opaque depth-writing cores. Original life colouring makes fresh particles scarlet and ageing curls and trails almost black. Birth lifetimes use the original 0.5–1.5 range. The current and previous view-projection matrices and particle position history measure actual screen travel; a bounded exposure elongates facets without altering compute animation. Nearby facets fade to prevent camera overdraw spikes. A separate GPU geometry/depth pass generates linear depth for occlusion and soft smoke intersections. Device or pipeline failure restores the compatible GPU renderer.

**WebGL compatibility:** The original floating-point FBO ping-pong simulation and GLSL curl helpers remain. The fallback uses the same world-space emitter, life-dependent follow force, movement-driven lifetime and turbulence, amorphous births, continuous idle curl and renewal, wall flattening and contact behavior. Current/previous position textures produce each particle's motion direction for smear, suppressing respawn spikes. Vertex texture fetch renders the same opaque scarlet-to-black triangle facets and depth-map intersections as WebGPU. Respawns suppress motion streaks; camera history includes orbit and follow movement in the shutter. This is GPU texture simulation; WebGPU uses native compute shaders.

The immutable eight-form atlas is shared by both paths. Auto starts at 16,384 active particles while preserving the original 65,536-particle atlas capacity. Changing the active prefix adjusts dispatch/draw counts without rebuilding the atlas or reading particle state. Newly reactivated slots respawn correctly, and form indexing keeps the capacity stride. Facet size compensates for smaller populations within a bounded scale. The WebGL fallback skips curl work for inactive texture cells and reduces the triangle draw range; it still uses the full allocated FBO.

## Rendering and performance

Third-person camera-relative movement, centred framing, smoothed mouse orbit and spring follow, wall/ceiling/prop collision, floor clearance, and restrained travel/aim FOV changes. Camera focus follows the moving plume, the arm follows the physical emitter and sprint/dash velocity, and the collision search retains at least 32 scene units of clearance in tested poses. Movement follows the visible camera direction instead of a hidden intended yaw. Pause, window focus loss, mimicry, wall attachment and aiming clear accumulated movement; pointer release outside the canvas clears dragging. A shortened camera arm searches all 32 horizontal sectors at two elevations for a comfortable, unobstructed view instead of zooming into the vapor. Wall mimicry anchors the camera to the original traversable player position. The dash validates the full existing stance through a surface-following 3D corridor, range and destination space; it has no airborne arc. A two-vertex direction line and small diamond mark the endpoint. Low form is locked for the transit, and both GPU paths translate the whole mass without a dash-specific animation. Downward ledge movement uses gravity; stairs follow sampled surfaces.

Charcoal architecture, ivory fixtures, restrained crimson accents and illustrated surface shading establish a dark graphic-novel style. `src/graphicNovel.js` uses four diffuse values, world-anchored hatching, panel seams, a restrained silhouette rim and one hard-shadow sample. It replaces the Phong, reflection, normal-map and bump-map materials on architecture, solid disguises and robotic enemies. The same pillars, pipework, server fronts, galleries, handrails, stairs, machinery, buildings and skyline retain their physical depth and cover. A depth-outline pass reuses the existing atmosphere pass, with FXAA afterward. The interface uses bold typography, paper-colored meters and ink panels without backdrop blur. Scarlet facets, dark curls and fine trailing fragments reproduce the original Spirit particle treatment shown in the reference video, including a continuously flowing amorphous idle cloud.

One following directional light renders a 512×512 hard shadow map. The three fixture point lights, two security spotlights and hemisphere light are removed from rendering. Security beams, fixture geometry and all lighting-sensitive stealth rules remain active; visibility/exposure still use the existing spatial occlusion tests. This is an illustrated lighting treatment, not a physically accurate reconstruction of fixture illumination. WebGPU particles share the scene camera and retain their own depth prepass; non-box details use approximate occlusion proxies.

Opaque walls and nested prop geometry are batched by material and spatial sector; static transforms are frozen. A deduplicated spatial grid retains exact 3D visibility and camera slab tests. Cone rays and consume eligibility refresh at 10 Hz, and dash corridors are cached while aiming and validated again on release. Uniforms and depth proxies reuse their buffers; HUD updates run at 12.5 Hz. Particle billboards use three triangle-list vertices and writable self depth, retaining the original curl octaves. This removes one vertex per WebGPU instance and the large translucent puff fragments. Shadows refresh at 20 Hz or immediately after a scene change. Packed RGBA8 scene depth and R16F particle proxy depth reduce color-attachment bandwidth. Render width is capped at 1600 pixels, and atmosphere depth uses 70% resolution. Speed blur shares the existing atmosphere pass: five extra color samples execute only while the travel or camera-orbit shutter is active, with reduced centre blur and camera-relative directional/peripheral streaks. Both particle renderers use the same travel direction and shutter strength for visual smear, retaining bounded facet exposure and near-camera fading and the ordinary compute animation. Dash lightning uses one reusable 216-vertex ribbon mesh with red edges and bright cores, depth-tested against the environment; it fades after arrival. No temporal image buffers, velocity-map passes or bloom are allocated; only one previous camera matrix is retained for particle exposure. The old full-frame motion-blur pipeline remains disabled.

Auto targets smooth 60 FPS by reducing active particles after sustained slow frames, down to 4K. Only if 4K remains slow does it lower render resolution toward 70%; sustained headroom restores resolution and then particles, up to 32K. Manual presets preserve their chosen count and reset resolution to full. No real-device 60+ FPS guarantee is made; native software GPU checks validate correctness, and local CPU gameplay checks do not measure browser GPU frame time.
